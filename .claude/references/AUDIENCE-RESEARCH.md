# Audience Research Method

## Start with situation, not persona decoration

Identify a priority group through a shared situation and sought progress:

- What happened that made the issue active now?
- What are they trying to accomplish?
- What do they do today?
- What is unsatisfactory about that approach?
- What outcome matters, and why?
- What risks, anxieties, habits, or constraints block change?
- What would make them trust an alternative?

Demographics are included only when they change need, access, language, trust, ability to buy, or delivery.

## Evidence inputs

Highest value:

- observed behavior and analytics;
- interviews about specific past events;
- sales, support, and onboarding conversations;
- cancellations, objections, and lost deals;
- search behavior and message tests;
- contextual customer reviews and public discussions.

Founder experience is useful context, but label it separately.

## Three research modes

Use the strongest mode available and keep their evidence roles separate:

1. **Provided evidence** — interviews, customer messages, analytics, sales or support notes, surveys, cancellations, and previous tests supplied by the participant.
2. **Public-signal research** — reviews, communities, search behavior, competitor pages, Q&A, and other public material used to identify situations, language, alternatives, and hypotheses.
3. **Direct research** — new interviews, observation, concierge tests, smoke tests, or real behavior captured after the workshop.

Public-signal research can make a hypothesis sharper and a website more relevant. It cannot establish prevalence, purchase intent, or demand for the proposed offer on its own. State which mode supports every material conclusion and what the next stronger mode should test.

## Jobs and progress layers

For each important situation, separate three kinds of progress:

- **Functional:** what the person needs to get done or change;
- **Emotional:** how they want to feel before, during, or after it;
- **Social:** how the choice affects how they are seen or how they relate to others.

Use only layers supported by evidence. Do not invent an emotional story merely to make the copy feel persuasive.

## Customer language rules

- Preserve exact wording only with a traceable source.
- Keep a short excerpt and source context; do not assemble fake composite quotes.
- Paraphrase when privacy, copyright, or length makes a quote inappropriate.
- Note whether the speaker is a buyer, user, influencer, or unknown.
- Do not assume a loud public complaint is common.

## Research matrix

For each important insight:

| Situation/trigger | Current behavior | Problem or tension | Desired progress | Barrier/objection | Trust cue | Language/source | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## Audience selection

Recommend one priority audience for the first website version. Evaluate:

- strength and urgency of the situation;
- fit with the offer's real capability;
- ability to reach and serve them;
- evidence that the problem exists;
- practical willingness or authority to act;
- ethical and strategic fit.

Do not manufacture a narrow segment when evidence supports a broader one. Do not use “everyone” as a usable audience.

Call the result an **evidence-backed priority audience** only when relevant behavior or need is supported by direct customer research, behavioral data, or multiple independent sources. Otherwise call it the **selected test audience** and preserve the evidence label.

Use this minimum decision record:

| Decision | Evidence strength | What supports it | What contradicts it | Safe to claim | Must still be tested |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

A founder anecdote can select a useful test audience but cannot establish prevalence, urgency, willingness to pay, or message resonance. Public reviews can reveal language and risk patterns, but do not establish demand for a new offer.

If relevant direct audience evidence is missing, the stage must produce a short validation plan: who to speak with, the past behavior to investigate, the riskiest assumption, and what evidence would change the audience choice.

## From insight to website content

Map research to content:

| Research insight | Website response |
|---|---|
| Trigger and desired progress | Headline and opening context |
| Current failed approach | Problem framing and comparison |
| Anxiety or perceived risk | Proof, FAQ, process, guarantee, or transparent limitation |
| Decision criteria | Benefits, mechanism, offer detail |
| Exact audience language | Vocabulary and examples, without copying long passages |
| Unknown or weak evidence | Research question or cautious copy |

## Interview follow-up

If direct research is needed, prefer questions about specific past behavior:

- “Tell me about the last time this happened.”
- “What did you try first?”
- “What made that difficult?”
- “What did you compare?”
- “What happened next?”

Avoid asking whether someone “would definitely buy” a hypothetical offer and treating the answer as demand.
