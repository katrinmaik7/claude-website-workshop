# Offer, Positioning, and Conversion Method

## Conversion premise

A conversion website helps the right visitor understand:

1. whether the offer is relevant to their situation;
2. what progress it helps them make;
3. how it works and what they receive;
4. why its claims are credible;
5. what it costs or requires;
6. what happens after the call to action;
7. what limitations or risks they should understand.

Conversion is not maximized by pressure alone. Clarity, fit, trust, and low-friction action protect both conversion quality and customer expectations.

## Offer design

An offer statement should specify:

- best-fit customer and situation;
- desired outcome;
- mechanism or experience;
- concrete deliverables;
- time, effort, price, or commitment;
- proof currently available;
- boundary: who or what it is not suitable for;
- single primary website action.

Never fill gaps with invented guarantees, results, scarcity, credentials, testimonials, or customer counts.

### Offer strength check

Before trying to improve wording, inspect the offer itself. Rate each item as `Strong evidence`, `Plausible hypothesis`, or `Weak/unknown`:

- the outcome is important to the selected audience;
- the mechanism makes the outcome believable;
- the time to first useful value is acceptable;
- the required effort, risk, and sacrifice are proportionate;
- the scope and deliverables are concrete;
- the difference matters against the real alternative;
- the business can deliver it consistently and economically.

Copy cannot rescue a weak or incoherent offer. If the largest gap is in the offer, return to the offer decision instead of adding stronger promises.

### Packaging and pricing discipline

Keep three decisions distinct:

1. **Package:** what is included, excluded, bundled, limited, or optional.
2. **Pricing metric:** what the customer pays per, when relevant.
3. **Price point:** the actual amount and terms.

Competitor prices are comparison evidence, not a target. Cost creates a practical floor; it does not by itself establish customer value. For an early offer, distinguish communication from validation. A participant-approved experiment price may be communicated with truthful framing such as `Proposed launch price`; it becomes price evidence only when the site captures a meaningful response to that price. Never present it as validated, viable, or currently purchasable when those facts are unknown.

## Desirability, feasibility, and viability gate

An attractive proposition is not yet a viable offer. Before approving an offer, separate three questions:

| Risk | Required question |
|---|---|
| Desirability | Is there evidence that the priority audience experiences this situation and cares enough to act? |
| Feasibility | Can the business source, create, deliver, support, and maintain the promise at the required quality and timing? |
| Viability | Can target revenue exceed the variable and delivery costs with an acceptable contribution after taxes, fees, refunds, and support? |

Create a compact feasibility and economics table:

| Item | Known fact or assumption | Evidence/source | Risk | What must be tested |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Cover at minimum:

- target price and whether it is cost-informed, competitor-informed, participant-set, or validated by behavior;
- competitor price range and what is included;
- materials or product cost;
- labor, delivery, platform/payment, tax, packaging, support, and refund cost drivers;
- supplier, capacity, timing, geographic, legal, and quality dependencies;
- a rough contribution range when inputs exist, or `Unknown — unit economics not yet testable` when they do not.

If price is materially below the observed market range, require an explicit cost or model rationale. If none exists, preserve it as an **experiment price**, not an approved viable price.

An experiment price may appear on a public page when the participant approves its truthful status and wording. Use framing such as `Proposed launch price` or `Expected price`, and keep economic uncertainty internal. If the page records no response, state internally that it communicates the proposition but does not validate willingness to pay. Omit the price only when it is genuinely undecided, unsafe to disclose, or intentionally excluded by the participant for a business reason.

Assign one offer status:

- **Evidence-backed offer** — desirability, feasibility, and viability have credible support.
- **Test offer** — one or more material assumptions remain; the website must run a real, measurable test.
- **Demo only** — the action cannot record behavior; the website cannot be described as market validation.

Participant approval cannot turn an unsupported assumption into evidence.

An unconnected destination does not automatically turn an approved `Test offer` into `Demo only`. Preserve the intended test as `Connection required before launch` unless the participant explicitly changes the objective after seeing which CTA, follow-up, and learning will be lost.

## Positioning sequence

1. Identify genuine alternatives, including the status quo.
2. Identify capabilities or approach that differ.
3. Translate them into value for a specific situation.
4. Select the customers who care most about that value.
5. Choose a market frame that makes the value easy to understand.
6. State proof and limitations.

Output a plain-language positioning decision, not a slogan.

## Message hierarchy

Use this order unless research supports another:

1. Relevance: situation and desired outcome.
2. Value: the most important credible benefit.
3. Mechanism: how the offer creates that value.
4. Proof: evidence appropriate to the claim.
5. Offer: what is included and required.
6. Objections: risk, fit, timing, price, effort.
7. Action: one clear next step.

## Proof matching

Match claim strength to proof:

| Claim | Suitable proof |
|---|---|
| Process or feature exists | Demonstration, screenshots, curriculum, specification |
| Customers value the experience | Attributed qualitative feedback with permission |
| Outcome occurs | Relevant results with context, sample, timeframe, and limits |
| Expertise | Verifiable work, credentials, publications, or track record |
| Demand or popularity | Accurate, current participation or usage data |

If proof does not exist, weaken the claim or state it as an intended outcome.

## Calls to action

- Use one primary action per page goal.
- State what happens next.
- Ask only for information needed at that step.
- Use visible labels and helpful validation.
- Avoid false urgency, preselected consent, hidden conditions, or ambiguous buttons.
- Prefer an action verb plus the result the visitor receives; add a qualifier only when it reduces a real uncertainty.

## Five-second clarity and message match

Before approving the page, check whether a first-time visitor can quickly answer:

- What is this?
- Is it for someone in my situation?
- What useful result or experience does it offer?
- Why should I believe or consider it?
- What happens if I take the primary action?

The first screen must also match the promise and awareness created by the expected traffic source. A visitor arriving from a specific event, search, recommendation, or campaign should not have to reinterpret a different message on the page.

## Conversion-event integrity

The primary action must produce an observable result that matches its label:

| CTA promise | Minimum real behavior |
|---|---|
| Join a waitlist | Contact information is actually stored, appropriate consent/privacy is handled, and a genuine success state is returned |
| Share a preference or vote | The structured response is actually persisted or sent to a working analytics/form endpoint |
| Book | A working calendar or request destination receives the booking |
| Buy or reserve | A real checkout/reservation system records the commitment and states terms |
| Demo or preview | Clearly says no submission, reservation, or list entry occurs |

If the action is not connected, change the language to an explicit prototype/demo state. Never display “you joined”, “saved”, “reserved”, “ordered”, or “confirmed” when nothing was persisted.

A public `Demo only` page should normally use navigation or exploration actions and request no input. Do not create a disposable preference form, fake order flow, fake validation, or fake confirmation merely to make the prototype feel interactive. A facilitated UX mock-up may demonstrate those states outside the public conversion journey, but it produces no demand evidence.

Free-text responses must not be sent to analytics as a substitute for a form backend. Use structured choices for anonymous event testing, or use a suitable form/data endpoint with appropriate privacy handling.

## Page plan

Every section in the structure must answer a visitor question or reduce a documented barrier. For each section define:

- visitor question;
- message;
- supporting evidence;
- desired action;
- missing asset or research gap.
